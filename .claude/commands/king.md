# King — Rewrite Copy the Way Stephen King Would Edit It

Rewrites portfolio copy using the principles from Stephen King's *On Writing*. Use when copy reads robotic, over-polished, corporate, or AI-generated — or when you want a page to sound like a person wrote it.

Pairs with `/noslop`. `/noslop` removes AI tells. `/king` adds human muscle. Run `/noslop` first to strip the slop, then `/king` to make what's left worth reading.

## The core belief

> "Writing is not life, but I think that sometimes it can be a way back to life."

King's whole method is anti-decoration. He believes the writer's job is to get an idea out of one head and into another without breaking it. Everything below serves that.

## Instructions

### The rules, in King's priority order

**1. Kill the adverbs. All of them, then put back the two that survive.**
> "The road to hell is paved with adverbs."

Adverbs are the writer flinching — propping up a verb that should stand on its own. "He closed the door firmly" → "He slammed the door." Attack `-ly` words first, then the intensifier family: *very, really, quite, incredibly, deeply, truly, genuinely, seriously, absolutely, completely, extremely, particularly, especially*.

If the verb needs the adverb, the verb is wrong. Change the verb.

**2. Second draft = first draft − 10%.**
King got this on a rejection slip and called it the best writing advice he ever received. It is not optional. Cut a tenth of the words and the piece gets faster, not thinner.

Cut first: throat-clearing openers, restatements, hedges (*sort of, kind of, a bit, somewhat, arguably, I think it's fair to say*), and any sentence that only sets up the next one.

**3. The passive voice is for cowards.**
> "I think timid writers like them for the same reason timid lovers like passive partners."

Passive voice hides who did it. On a portfolio that's fatal — the whole point is that *you* did it. "The strategy was developed" → "I built the strategy." "It was decided that" → "We decided." Name the actor. Every time.

**4. Vocabulary: use the first word that comes to mind, if it's vivid.**
> "One of the really bad things you can do to your writing is to dress up the vocabulary, looking for long words because you're maybe a little bit ashamed of your short ones."

Don't reach for the thesaurus. *Utilize* → *use*. *Facilitate* → *help*. *Leverage* → *use*. *Commence* → *start*. Short Anglo-Saxon words carry more weight than long Latinate ones.

**5. Paragraphs are the beat of the piece.**
> "I would argue that the paragraph, not the sentence, is the basic unit of writing."

Vary them. A one-line paragraph lands like a punch — but only if the paragraphs around it are longer. Three short paragraphs in a row is a stutter, not emphasis.

**6. Dialogue and quotes tell the truth about people.**
Use the real quote, in the person's real register. Never smooth a quote into brand voice. If someone said "that's insane," don't print "that's remarkable."

**7. Description: two or three details, chosen well.**
> "Description begins in the writer's imagination but should finish in the reader's."

Don't inventory. Pick the detail that does the most work and trust the reader for the rest. A 103 mph fastball and a seven-hour rain delay tells you more about baseball than a paragraph of adjectives.

**8. No fear.**
> "Write with the door closed, rewrite with the door open."

The first pass is for getting the truth down. Timid copy — hedged, credit-sharing to the point of vanishing, afraid to say the campaign missed — is worse than blunt copy. Say the real thing.

### What this means for a portfolio specifically

- **Say what you did.** Not "contributed to," not "was part of a team that." What did *you* do?
- **Numbers beat adjectives.** "26.4M impressions" beats "massive reach." Always.
- **Admit what missed.** A case study that only wins reads like a brochure. "Sign-ups missed. Organic impressions came in under goal." That sentence buys credibility for every claim around it.
- **Cut the summary paragraph at the end.** The reader was just there.
- **One idea per sentence.** If you need a semicolon, you probably need a period.

### Workflow

1. Read the full page before changing a word. Know what it's trying to do.
2. Note 3–5 things about the writer's actual voice worth keeping — rhythm, bluntness, humor, the way they open a section. Keep this internal.
3. Pass one — **cut**: adverbs, passive constructions, dressed-up vocabulary, hedges, restatement.
4. Pass two — **strengthen**: weak verbs → strong verbs, abstractions → specifics, vague claims → numbers.
5. Pass three — **rhythm**: read it aloud in your head. Vary paragraph length. Fix anything you stumble on.
6. Cut 10% from what you now have.
7. Run the checklist below. Fix anything that fails, then check again.
8. Output the rewritten copy plus a short **What changed** section.

### Checklist

**Cutting**
1. Are the adverbs gone except where one genuinely changes the meaning?
2. Is the draft at least 10% shorter than what you started with?
3. Are hedges and throat-clearing removed?
4. Is every sentence load-bearing — would removing it lose something?

**Strengthening**
5. Is every sentence active, with a named actor?
6. Are weak verb phrases replaced with direct verbs ("made a decision" → "decided")?
7. Is dressed-up vocabulary swapped for the plain word?
8. Are vague claims replaced with numbers, names, or dates?
9. Do descriptions use two or three chosen details instead of an inventory?

**Rhythm and truth**
10. Do paragraph lengths vary, with short ones used sparingly enough to land?
11. Are quotes in the speaker's real register, unsmoothed?
12. Does the piece admit anything that didn't work?
13. Does it end on the last concrete point, with no summary recap?
14. Read aloud, does it sound like a person talking, not a brand?
15. Would the writer recognize this as their own voice, just sharper?

### Output

Return the rewritten copy, then a short **What changed** section naming the specific moves (adverbs cut, passives flipped, X% shorter). If the source had a claim you couldn't verify or a number that looked wrong, flag it rather than rewriting around it.
